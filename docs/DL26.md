# DL26: A Decade Later

The site's second course, and the continuation of CS231n. CS231n (Winter 2016) ends just
before deep learning changed shape. DL26 covers what came after: attention and transformers,
language-model pretraining, scale, modern optimizers, vision transformers, contrastive
learning and diffusion, and post-training with feedback (RLHF, PPO, DPO, RLVR, GRPO),
reasoning models and fast inference. Course id `dl26`, route `#/dl26`.

Chapters follow `docs/AUTHORING.md` in every way except its "The course" section, which this
file replaces for DL26. Lectures follow `docs/LECTURES.md`.

## Audience and voice

The reader finished CS231n here (or knows its material): they know linear classifiers,
softmax and cross-entropy, backprop, SGD and Adam, ConvNets and RNNs. Their math still stops
at Calculus 1, a long time ago, so every equation is read out in plain words and every new
mathematical object (expectation, log-probability, KL divergence, matrix rank, orthogonal
matrix, power law) is introduced briefly where it first matters. Link back to CS231n chapters
when leaning on them: `#/math-kit`, `#/backprop`, `#/nn-learning` (optimizers), `#/rnn`,
`#/attention`, `#/unsupervised`.

The goal is comprehensive and insightful: not a list of techniques, but why each one was
needed, what problem it solved, what it costs, and the one picture that makes it obvious.
Prefer mechanisms over history, but give each idea its paper and year.

## Code

Listings are Python. Use NumPy wherever the computation fits (attention, BPE, sampling,
scaling-law fits, Newton-Schulz, LoRA, PPO and GRPO losses, diffusion on 2-D points). Where
the idea is about training real models (parallelism, FSDP, mixed precision, chat templates),
PyTorch-style Python is fine; keep it short and correct. Figures compute in the browser in
TypeScript at toy scale: a two-layer transformer with a 6-word vocabulary, a 2-D diffusion
model, a 2x2 or 8x8 matrix for Muon. Never fake a result a figure claims to compute; if a
figure shows published numbers (a scaling-law plot, a benchmark), say where they come from
and keep them few.

## Accuracy

This is a course. Formulas, constants, dates and attributions must be right. If unsure of a
detail (an exact benchmark score, a hyperparameter in one paper), say less: describe the
qualitative result. Well-established facts to get right: Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017),
BERT and GPT (2018), GPT-2 (2019), GPT-3 and the Kaplan scaling laws (2020), ViT, DDPM and
SimCLR (2020), CLIP and LoRA (2021), Chinchilla, InstructGPT and FlashAttention (2022),
DPO (2023), GRPO in DeepSeekMath (2024), Muon (Keller Jordan, late 2024; Moonshot's
large-scale study, 2025), DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025).

## Chapters

**0 `decade`: What changed after 2016.** A timeline from 2017 to 2026 drawn as a figure
(tap a year: what appeared, why it mattered). The three big shifts: one architecture for
everything (attention), scale as a research direction (pretraining, scaling laws), and
learning from feedback (RLHF to RLVR). What carried over from CS231n unchanged (backprop,
SGD, softmax, cross-entropy, residual connections, normalization). How to read this course.

**1 `seq2seq`: From recurrence to attention.** Encoder-decoder RNNs for translation and the
fixed-vector bottleneck (show quality falling with sentence length qualitatively). Bahdanau
attention: score every encoder state, softmax, weighted sum. Attention as a soft dictionary
lookup: queries, keys, values; hard versus soft lookup. Dot-product versus additive scores.
Figure: an alignment matrix for a short sentence pair; a soft lookup you can steer by moving
a query vector in 2-D.

**2 `transformer`: The Transformer.** Self-attention with Q, K, V projections; scaled dot
product and why divide by sqrt(d) (variance of a dot product grows with d; show the softmax
saturating without it). Multi-head attention (heads attend to different things; concat and
project). Causal masking for decoders. Positional information: attention is permutation
invariant without it; sinusoidal encodings. The block: attention plus MLP, residual
connections, layer norm. Encoder, decoder, encoder-decoder. Parameter count of a block
(about 12 d^2). Figures: attention weights on a sentence with a head selector; the effect of
the sqrt(d) scaling on softmax sharpness; a block diagram whose parts light up with the code.

**3 `tokenization`: Tokens and embeddings.** Characters, words, subwords; the vocabulary-size
versus sequence-length tradeoff. Byte-pair encoding step by step (count pairs, merge the most
frequent, repeat), byte-level BPE, encode and decode. Tokenization quirks (numbers, spaces,
other languages). Embedding tables, the unembedding (the output projection back to
vocabulary logits) and weight tying. Figures: a live BPE trainer on a tiny corpus with a merge
slider; a 2-D embedding space.

**4 `pretraining`: Language models and pretraining.** Next-token prediction and the chain rule
of probability; cross-entropy per token and perplexity; teacher forcing. Sampling: greedy,
temperature, top-k, top-p (nucleus). GPT (causal) versus BERT (masked) objectives. Why
pretraining on the web works: compression, in-context learning, few-shot prompting (GPT-3).
Figures: a tiny n-gram or tiny-model next-token distribution with temperature and top-p
sliders; perplexity as the effective number of choices.

**5 `scaling-laws`: Scaling laws and compute.** Power laws and why they look straight on log
axes. Loss versus parameters N, data D and compute C (Kaplan et al., 2020). Training FLOPs
about 6ND (2 for forward, 4 for backward, per parameter per token). Chinchilla (2022):
compute-optimal training uses roughly 20 tokens per parameter; earlier models were
under-trained. Training beyond compute-optimal for cheaper inference. Figures: a compute
budget slider showing the optimal N and D; fitting a power law to points in log space.

**6 `modern-transformer`: The modern transformer block.** Pre-norm versus post-norm and why
pre-norm trains deeper stacks; RMSNorm. Rotary position embeddings (RoPE): rotate query and
key pairs by position, so their dot product depends on relative offset. SwiGLU feed-forward.
Multi-query and grouped-query attention to shrink the KV cache. The KV cache itself (memory
per token = 2 x layers x kv_heads x head_dim x bytes). FlashAttention: the softmax can be
computed in tiles with a running max and sum (online softmax), so the full attention matrix
never touches slow memory. Figures: RoPE rotating 2-D pairs as position changes; online
softmax over tiles matching the direct result; KV-cache memory calculator.

**7 `optimizers`: Optimizers at scale: AdamW to Muon.** Adam recap (link `#/nn-learning`).
L2 regularization versus decoupled weight decay (AdamW) and why they differ under Adam.
Learning-rate warmup and cosine decay; warmup-stable-decay. Gradient clipping by global norm.
Mixed precision: fp16 and bf16 ranges, loss scaling, fp32 master weights. Muon: for 2-D weight
matrices, take the momentum matrix and replace it with the nearest semi-orthogonal matrix
(all singular values set to 1), computed with a few Newton-Schulz iterations instead of an
SVD; intuition: equalize the update across directions so rare directions are not drowned out;
used for hidden matrices, with AdamW kept for embeddings, the output layer and gains. Mention
Shampoo and SOAP as relatives, and muP for transferring hyperparameters across widths.
Figures: Newton-Schulz iterations driving singular values of a small matrix toward 1;
optimizer race on a 2-D ill-conditioned problem (SGD, Adam, and an orthogonalized update);
warmup plus cosine schedule.

**8 `parallelism`: Training across many GPUs.** Memory accounting for training with Adam in
mixed precision (about 16 bytes per parameter before activations); activations and
checkpointing. Data parallelism and all-reduce. ZeRO stages and FSDP (shard optimizer states,
gradients, parameters). Tensor parallelism (split matrices across devices) and pipeline
parallelism (stages, micro-batches, the bubble). Figures: a memory calculator; an animated
pipeline schedule showing the bubble shrink with more micro-batches; ring all-reduce.

**9 `moe`: Mixture of experts.** Replace one feed-forward block with E experts and a router;
top-k gating (softmax over router logits, keep k). Total versus active parameters. Load
balancing (auxiliary loss, capacity factor, dropped tokens), fine-grained and shared experts
(DeepSeekMoE), expert parallelism. Figures: tokens routed to experts with a load histogram
and a balancing-loss slider; total versus active parameter calculator.

**10 `vit`: Vision transformers.** Split an image into 16x16 patches, flatten, project, add
position embeddings and a class token, run a transformer encoder. Inductive bias: ConvNets
assume locality and translation equivariance; ViT has to learn them, so it needs more data
(or distillation and augmentation, DeiT). Hybrid ideas (Swin windows) briefly. Figures: a
CIFAR image cut into patches and tokens; attention from the class token over patches.

**11 `contrastive`: Self-supervised learning and CLIP.** Learning without labels. Contrastive
learning: two augmented views of an image should match each other and not other images;
InfoNCE as softmax cross-entropy over a batch; temperature. SimCLR, MoCo's queue. CLIP:
image and text encoders trained so matching pairs have high cosine similarity; zero-shot
classification by comparing an image with prompts like "a photo of a dog". Masked
autoencoders (MAE) and self-distillation (DINO) as the other two families. Figures: a batch
similarity matrix with the diagonal as targets; 2-D embeddings pulled together and pushed
apart as training runs; zero-shot classification with editable prompts on a toy embedding.

**12 `diffusion`: Diffusion models.** Forward process: add Gaussian noise in small steps; the
closed form x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t) x_0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) eps. Train a network to predict
the noise (a simple mean squared error). Sampling: start from noise, denoise step by step
(DDPM), fewer steps with DDIM. Score intuition: the denoiser points toward the data.
Classifier-free guidance. Latent diffusion (work in an autoencoder's latent space) and
diffusion transformers. Flow matching and rectified flow as straight-line paths. Figures: a 2-D
dataset (a spiral or two moons) noised by a slider and denoised by a small MLP trained in the
browser; the learned vector field; guidance strength slider.

**13 `finetuning`: Fine-tuning, instruction tuning and LoRA.** From a base model to an
assistant: supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, chat templates, masking the prompt so
loss is only on the response. Catastrophic forgetting. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning: LoRA
(W + B A with rank r much smaller than d; B starts at zero; scale alpha / r), merging after
training, QLoRA (4-bit base weights). Figures: rank-r approximation of a matrix with a rank
slider (parameter savings versus error); which tokens carry loss in a chat example.

**14 `policy-gradients`: Reinforcement learning for language models.** Generation as a policy:
state is the text so far, action is the next token, a reward at the end. Expected reward and
the policy gradient: grad E[R] = E[R grad log pi(y)], the log-derivative trick derived
gently. REINFORCE, variance, baselines and advantages. The KL penalty to a reference model
to stay close to good language. Figures: a bandit over a few answers whose probabilities shift
under REINFORCE, with and without a baseline (variance visible); KL leash strength.

**15 `rlhf`: RLHF and PPO.** Preference data (pairs: chosen and rejected). Reward model with
the Bradley-Terry loss: -log sigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected). PPO: importance ratio, the
clipped surrogate objective and why clipping keeps steps small; value function, advantages
and GAE; the per-token KL penalty; the four models in memory (policy, reference, reward,
value). Reward hacking and over-optimization. InstructGPT as the canonical recipe. Figures:
the clipped objective as a function of the ratio for positive and negative advantages; reward
model training on toy pairs; over-optimization curve (proxy reward up, true quality down).

**16 `dpo`: Direct preference optimization.** Start from the KL-regularized RL objective; its
optimal policy is pi_ref times exp(r / beta), normalized; invert to express reward through
the policy; substitute into Bradley-Terry: the DPO loss
-log sigmoid(beta (log ratio chosen - log ratio rejected)). Implicit reward; the role of
beta. Strengths (simple, stable, offline) and limits (no exploration, distribution shift).
Variants named briefly (IPO, KTO, SimPO). Figures: DPO loss and gradient weight versus the
implicit reward margin; a toy policy over four answers trained with DPO.

**17 `grpo`: RL from verifiable rewards and GRPO.** Rewards that a program can check (a math
answer, unit tests): no reward model to hack. GRPO (DeepSeekMath, 2024): sample a group of G
answers per prompt, advantage = (reward - group mean) / group std, PPO-style clipped
objective, no value network, KL to the reference. DeepSeek-R1-Zero and R1 (2025): pure RL on
verifiable tasks grows long chains of thought and self-verification; then distillation into
small models. Known issues and fixes: length bias from per-sequence normalization, all-correct
or all-wrong groups give zero signal, entropy collapse (Dr. GRPO, DAPO named briefly).
Figures: a group of sampled answers with rewards and their advantages; a toy RLVR run where
accuracy and answer length rise together.

**18 `reasoning`: Reasoning and test-time compute.** Chain-of-thought prompting; why writing
intermediate steps helps a fixed-depth network. Self-consistency (sample many, vote).
Best-of-n with a verifier; outcome versus process reward models. Scaling test-time compute:
accuracy versus samples or thinking length. Reasoning models trained with RL (o1, R1) and
distilled reasoning. Figures: majority voting accuracy as samples grow, from per-sample
accuracy p; best-of-n with a noisy verifier.

**19 `inference`: Fast inference.** Prefill (parallel, compute-bound) versus decode (one token
at a time, memory-bound); arithmetic intensity. The KV cache and its memory; paged attention;
continuous batching. Quantization: int8 and int4 weights, scales and zero points, outliers.
Speculative decoding: a small draft model proposes k tokens, the big model verifies them in
one pass; the accept or resample rule keeps the output distribution exact. Distillation.
Figures: tokens per second versus batch size (memory-bound to compute-bound); quantization
error for a weight histogram at 8, 4 and 2 bits; speculative decoding acceptance simulation.

**20 `interpretability`: Looking inside language models.** The residual stream as a shared
channel that layers read from and write to. Attention heads as moving information; induction
heads (copy what followed the last time this token appeared) and in-context learning. Linear
probes. Superposition: more features than dimensions. Sparse autoencoders that decompose
activations into interpretable features. Figures: an induction-head pattern on a repeated
random sequence; a toy superposition model (features as arrows in 2-D) with a sparsity slider.
